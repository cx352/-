
Nutrition Science Knowledge Base
1. Purpose
This module defines the evidence-based nutrition framework for natural bodybuilding.
It supports:
• muscle gain
• fat loss
• recomposition
• contest preparation
• post-contest recovery
• long-term bodyweight management
• training performance
• recovery
The system should not prescribe calories or macronutrients from bodyweight alone.
Nutrition decisions must consider:
• bodyweight trend
• training performance
• activity
• recovery
• body composition
• current phase
• adherence
• individual response
Core principle:
Nutrition should provide sufficient energy and nutrients to support the athlete's current goal while remaining sustainable.
--------------------
2. Energy Balance
Changes in body mass are primarily influenced by the relationship between:
• energy intake
• energy expenditure
However, real-world bodyweight is also affected by:
• glycogen
• water
• sodium
• food volume
• gastrointestinal contents
• inflammation
Therefore:
Short-term bodyweight change should not automatically be interpreted as fat or muscle change.
Use multi-day and multi-week trends.
--------------------
3. Maintenance Calories
Maintenance calories are not a fixed biological number.
They depend on:
• body size
• lean mass
• training
• steps
• cardio
• occupation
• spontaneous activity
• food intake
• adaptive changes
The most useful practical estimate is:
Start with an estimated maintenance intake and refine it using actual bodyweight trends.
--------------------
4. Calorie Estimation
Initial calorie estimates can use:
• bodyweight
• height
• age
• sex
• estimated activity
• training frequency
Common equations may provide a starting estimate.
However:
Measured longitudinal bodyweight response should eventually take priority over an equation-based estimate.
Example:
If estimated maintenance is 2,800 kcal but the athlete maintains bodyweight at approximately 3,000 kcal over several weeks, the observed intake-response relationship is more useful for individual planning.
--------------------
5. Muscle-Gain Calories
Muscle gain generally benefits from adequate energy availability.
Possible strategies:
Conservative Gain
Small calorie surplus.
Useful when:
• minimizing fat gain is important
• the athlete is relatively advanced
• the athlete wants a long gaining phase
Standard Gain
Moderate surplus.
Useful when:
• muscle gain is the primary goal
• the athlete has sufficient recovery capacity
Higher-Calorie Gain
Larger surplus.
Potentially useful in selected circumstances, but usually accompanied by greater fat gain.
The system should not assume that a larger surplus produces proportionally greater muscle growth.
--------------------
6. Weight-Gain Rate
A practical starting framework for gaining is approximately:
0.10–0.25% of bodyweight per week.
This is not a universal requirement.
The appropriate rate depends on:
• training age
• current body composition
• genetic response
• previous gaining history
• appetite
• recovery
• desired rate of fat gain
Advanced athletes may benefit from slower rates.
--------------------
7. Fat-Loss Calories
Fat loss requires a sustained energy deficit.
The appropriate deficit depends on:
• starting body-fat level
• training status
• timeline
• recovery
• competition requirements
A practical starting approach is:
Use the smallest calorie deficit that produces the desired rate of progress.
Avoid unnecessarily aggressive deficits when muscle retention is a priority.
--------------------
8. Recomposition
Recomposition describes simultaneous improvement in:
• muscle mass
• body composition
It can be more achievable in:
• beginners
• detrained individuals
• individuals with higher body-fat levels
• athletes returning from a break
• individuals with substantial room for training adaptation
It may also occur in trained athletes, although the rate is generally slower.
Monitor:
• performance
• waist
• bodyweight
• visual changes
Do not judge recomposition using bodyweight alone.
--------------------
9. Protein
Protein is a central nutritional variable for resistance-trained athletes.
A practical general range:
Approximately 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day.
Higher protein intakes may sometimes be useful when:
• calories are restricted
• the athlete is very lean
• hunger is high
• maintaining muscle is a major priority
Protein should be interpreted in the context of total calories and dietary adherence.
--------------------
10. Protein Distribution
Protein can be distributed across multiple meals.
A practical strategy is to consume several protein-containing meals throughout the day rather than relying on one very large serving.
Useful considerations:
• total daily protein
• meal distribution
• food quality
• digestion
• preference
• adherence
Total daily intake remains the primary variable.
--------------------
11. Carbohydrates
Carbohydrates provide:
• training fuel
• glycogen
• dietary energy
• food flexibility
Carbohydrate requirements vary according to:
• training volume
• training frequency
• body size
• cardio
• daily activity
• calorie intake
• preference
Higher training workloads generally create greater carbohydrate demands.
--------------------
12. Carbohydrate and Training Performance
Carbohydrate availability can influence:
• high-volume training
• repeated sets
• perceived energy
• glycogen availability
• recovery
If performance deteriorates:
evaluate:
1. total calories
2. carbohydrate
3. sleep
4. recovery
5. training fatigue
6. activity
Do not automatically increase training volume.
--------------------
13. Fat
Dietary fat provides:
• energy
• essential fatty acids
• dietary flexibility
• food palatability
Avoid unnecessarily extreme fat restriction.
A practical approach:
7. establish sufficient protein
8. maintain an appropriate fat intake
9. allocate remaining calories according to training needs and preference
--------------------
14. Carbohydrate vs Fat Distribution
There is no universal carbohydrate-to-fat ratio for all natural bodybuilders.
Consider:
• training demands
• preference
• digestion
• appetite
• performance
• dietary adherence
Higher carbohydrate strategies may be useful for high-volume training.
Higher-fat strategies may suit individuals who prefer lower carbohydrate intake.
The decision should be individualized.
--------------------
15. Fiber
Fiber supports:
• digestive health
• satiety
• food quality
However, extremely high fiber intake may create:
• excessive food volume
• gastrointestinal discomfort
• reduced appetite
During contest preparation, fiber may need individual adjustment according to digestive tolerance and stage-day requirements.
--------------------
16. Food Quality
A bodybuilding diet should generally include:
• protein-rich foods
• carbohydrate-rich foods
• vegetables
• fruits
• dietary fats
• adequate micronutrient sources
Food quality supports:
• micronutrient intake
• satiety
• digestion
• dietary variety
However:
Body composition is not determined by "clean food" versus "dirty food" alone.
Total calorie intake and macronutrient intake remain central.
--------------------
17. Flexible Dieting
Dietary adherence is an important determinant of long-term success.
A sustainable diet can include a range of foods provided that:
• calorie intake is appropriate
• protein is sufficient
• micronutrient needs are reasonably covered
• dietary fiber is adequate
• food choices remain compatible with the goal
Rigid food rules are not automatically superior.
--------------------
18. Meal Frequency
Meal frequency has relatively limited direct importance for hypertrophy when total calories and protein are adequately controlled.
Meal frequency can still influence:
• hunger
• digestion
• training timing
• convenience
• adherence
Therefore:
Choose a meal frequency that supports performance and adherence.
--------------------
19. Pre-Workout Nutrition
Pre-workout nutrition may help provide:
• energy
• carbohydrate availability
• amino acids
Useful considerations:
• time before training
• meal size
• carbohydrate amount
• protein amount
• digestive tolerance
Large meals immediately before training may be uncomfortable for some athletes.
--------------------
20. Post-Workout Nutrition
Post-workout nutrition contributes to:
• recovery
• protein intake
• glycogen restoration
The athlete does not need to consume protein immediately after the final repetition.
Overall daily nutrition remains more important.
Post-workout carbohydrate becomes more relevant when:
• training volume is high
• another session is scheduled soon
• glycogen restoration is a priority
--------------------
21. Hydration
Hydration influences:
• performance
• recovery
• bodyweight
• appearance
Monitor:
• fluid intake
• sweat losses
• training environment
• sodium intake
Bodyweight changes caused by hydration should not automatically be interpreted as changes in tissue mass.
--------------------
22. Sodium
Sodium influences:
• fluid balance
• blood volume
• performance
• bodyweight
Large short-term sodium changes can alter scale weight.
During normal training:
Consistent sodium intake can make bodyweight trends easier to interpret.
During contest preparation:
avoid unnecessary extreme sodium manipulation.
--------------------
23. Creatine
Creatine monohydrate is one of the most extensively studied sports supplements.
Potential benefits include:
• improved high-intensity performance
• increased training capacity
• increased lean mass over time
A common maintenance strategy is approximately:
3–5 g/day.
Loading is optional.
Creatine may increase bodyweight through increased intracellular water.
This does not automatically represent fat gain.
--------------------
24. Supplements
Supplements should be evaluated according to:
10. evidence
11. safety
12. necessity
13. cost
14. individual response
The system should prioritize:
• calories
• protein
• carbohydrate
• dietary fat
• micronutrients
• hydration
• sleep
before extensive supplementation.
--------------------
25. Caffeine
Caffeine may improve:
• alertness
• perceived effort
• exercise performance
Response varies between individuals.
Potential disadvantages:
• sleep disruption
• anxiety
• tolerance
• dependence on stimulants
Avoid using caffeine to compensate for chronic sleep deprivation.
--------------------
26. Energy Availability
Energy availability refers to energy remaining after exercise expenditure relative to body size and physiological needs.
Very low energy availability can negatively affect:
• recovery
• training performance
• physiological function
• long-term health
Risk becomes more important during:
• aggressive dieting
• very lean contest preparation
• high training volume
• high cardio
• excessive daily activity
The system should recognize persistent low-energy states as a potential recovery problem.
--------------------
27. Bulking Decision Engine
When the athlete is gaining:
Monitor:
• 7-day average weight
• 14-day trend
• waist
• performance
• recovery
Weight rising slowly + performance improving
Maintain.
Weight not changing + performance stable
Consider whether additional calories are actually required.
Weight not changing + performance declining
Investigate:
• calories
• carbohydrate
• sleep
• training fatigue
• activity
Weight rising rapidly + waist rising rapidly
Consider reducing the surplus.
Do not automatically continue increasing calories.
--------------------
28. Cutting Decision Engine
During fat loss:
Weight falling at desired rate
Maintain.
Weight falling too slowly
Check:
• adherence
• calorie accuracy
• steps
• cardio
• water fluctuations
Then make a small adjustment.
Weight falling too quickly + performance declining
Consider:
• increasing calories
• reducing cardio
• reducing activity
• reducing the size of the deficit
--------------------
29. Recomposition Decision Engine
Possible successful recomposition pattern:
• bodyweight stable
• waist decreasing
• performance improving
Another possible pattern:
• bodyweight slowly decreasing
• waist decreasing
• performance maintained
Do not automatically lower calories further if body composition is improving.
--------------------
30. Weight-Change Interpretation
Use moving averages.
Short-term weight increase
Potential causes:
• glycogen
• sodium
• water
• food volume
• inflammation
Long-term weight increase
More likely to reflect:
• increased tissue mass
• sustained energy surplus
But body composition still requires additional indicators.
--------------------
31. Activity Adjustment
Energy expenditure can change without deliberate dietary changes.
Monitor:
• steps
• cardio
• occupation
• spontaneous activity
Example:
If food remains constant but daily steps increase substantially:
effective energy balance may shift toward a larger deficit.
Therefore:
Activity must be included in calorie adjustment decisions.
--------------------
32. Dietary Adherence
Before changing calorie targets:
verify actual intake.
Potential causes of discrepancy:
• inaccurate food weighing
• restaurant meals
• sauces
• cooking oils
• snacks
• beverages
• weekend eating
• inconsistent portion sizes
Do not assume the calculated calorie target equals actual intake.
--------------------
33. Raw vs Cooked Food Weight
Food tracking must clearly distinguish:
• raw weight
• cooked weight
• edible portion
Examples:
• raw rice
• cooked rice
• raw meat
• cooked meat
The system should never mix raw and cooked weights when calculating calories.
--------------------
34. Nutrition Data Quality
Classify nutrition information as:
DOCUMENTED
Directly measured or clearly recorded.
ATHLETE-REPORTED
Reported by the athlete.
ESTIMATED
Calculated from known food quantities.
DERIVED
Calculated from other information.
UNKNOWN
Insufficient information.
Do not present estimates as exact values.
--------------------
35. Individual Response
Different athletes can respond differently to the same calorie intake.
Differences may arise from:
• activity
• lean mass
• genetics
• appetite
• digestion
• training volume
• spontaneous movement
• metabolic adaptation
Therefore:
Actual longitudinal response should be used to calibrate the plan.
--------------------
36. Nutrition Adjustment Size
Prefer small changes.
A practical starting adjustment:
Approximately 100–250 kcal/day.
The exact adjustment depends on:
• body size
• current intake
• weight trend
• training
• activity
• recovery
Avoid repeated large changes.
--------------------

38. Nutrition × Training Interaction
Nutrition decisions cannot be separated completely from training.
Examples:
High Training Volume + High Activity
May require more carbohydrate and/or total calories.
Low Training Volume + Low Activity
May require fewer calories.
Calorie Deficit + High Training Volume
Greater recovery risk.
Calorie Surplus + Low Activity
Greater risk of unnecessary fat gain if the surplus is excessive.
Therefore:
Nutrition must be matched to total workload and the current goal.
--------------------
39. Post-Contest Nutrition
Immediately after competition:
bodyweight can rise rapidly because of:
• glycogen restoration
• increased carbohydrate
• increased sodium
• increased food volume
• increased gut contents
• water restoration
Do not automatically interpret this as rapid fat gain.
Monitor:
• 7-day average
• waist
• visual condition
• training performance
• hunger
• recovery
--------------------
40. Natural Bodybuilding Application
Natural bodybuilding nutrition should prioritize:
• sustainable calorie control
• adequate protein
• sufficient carbohydrate for training
• sufficient dietary fat
• recovery
• adherence
The system should not rely on:
• extreme restriction
• unnecessary supplement stacks
• extreme carbohydrate manipulation
• repeated crash diets
--------------------
41. Evidence Hierarchy
Nutrition recommendations should prioritize:
Level 1
Systematic reviews and meta-analyses
Level 2
Controlled human trials
Level 3
Longitudinal athlete data
Level 4
Expert practice
Level 5
Anecdotal reports
When athlete practice conflicts with strong scientific evidence:
investigate the context rather than automatically choosing either source.

45. Core Rules
15. Energy balance drives long-term bodyweight change.
16. Short-term scale changes are heavily influenced by water and glycogen.
17. Protein should generally remain high during gaining and cutting.
18. Carbohydrate should support training demands.
19. Dietary fat should not be unnecessarily restricted.
20. Calorie targets are estimates until validated by longitudinal data.
21. Activity changes can substantially alter energy expenditure.
22. Raw and cooked food weights must not be mixed.
23. Adherence must be verified before major calorie changes.
24. Small adjustments are preferable to repeated large changes.
25. Recomposition should be evaluated using multiple indicators.
26. Post-contest weight gain is not automatically fat gain.
27. Supplements should support, not replace, the fundamentals.
28. Individual response should calibrate the nutrition plan.
29. Natural athlete case studies are practical references, not causal proof.
30. Scientific evidence takes priority over anecdotal practice.
Final principle:
The best bodybuilding diet is not the diet with the most precise theoretical numbers. It is the diet whose actual energy and nutrient intake produces the desired body-composition trend while supporting training, recovery, health, and long-term adherence.
