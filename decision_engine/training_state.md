
Training State Engine
1. Purpose
This module determines the athlete's current training state and converts that state into practical training decisions.
The system must not assume that one training split, frequency, volume, intensity, or proximity-to-failure strategy works for every athlete.
Training recommendations must be determined by the interaction between:
• training age
• current performance
• recent training load
• bodyweight trend
• nutrition
• recovery
• daily activity
• fatigue
• target muscle response
• current phase
• individual tolerance
Core principle:
Training is a stimulus-recovery-adaptation system.
The goal is not to maximize training stress.
The goal is to maximize productive training stimulus that the athlete can recover from and progressively adapt to.
--------------------
2. Core Training State Model
The system classifies the athlete into one or more dominant states.
Primary states:
1. Productive Training
2. Productive Gaining
3. Recomposition Training
4. Energy-Limited Training
5. Recovery-Limited Training
6. Plateau / Adaptation-Limited Training
7. Deload / Fatigue-Management State
These states are not permanent categories.
An athlete can move between states from week to week.

4. Productive Training State
Definition
The athlete is recovering adequately and producing measurable training progress.
Typical signs:
• performance stable or improving
• repetitions improving
• load improving
• technique improving
• target-muscle stimulus remains strong
• recovery acceptable
• soreness manageable
• sleep acceptable
• motivation acceptable
• bodyweight trend consistent with current phase
Training Decision
Do not automatically increase volume.
First determine whether the existing training stimulus is already producing adaptation.
If performance is improving and recovery is acceptable:
Maintain the current training structure unless there is a specific reason to change it.
Possible progression:
8. increase repetitions
9. improve execution
10. improve ROM
11. increase load
12. add a set only when justified
--------------------
5. Productive Gaining State
Definition
The athlete is in a calorie-surplus or maintenance-to-surplus environment and is experiencing productive training adaptation.
Typical pattern:
• bodyweight gradually increasing
• waist relatively controlled
• performance improving
• recovery good
• training quality high
• target muscles responding
Training Strategy
The primary objective is not to maximize fatigue.
The objective is to maximize recoverable hypertrophy stimulus.
Maintain:
• adequate training frequency
• sufficient weekly volume
• high-quality exercise execution
• progressive overload
• appropriate RIR
• adequate recovery
Increase training volume only when:
• performance is no longer progressing
• recovery remains good
• target muscle stimulus is insufficient
• adherence is high
• nutrition is adequate
Do not add volume simply because the athlete is bulking.
--------------------
6. Recomposition Training State
Definition
The athlete is attempting to gain or maintain muscle while reducing body fat.
Typical pattern:
• bodyweight stable or slowly changing
• waist decreasing or stable
• performance stable or improving
• visual physique improving
• recovery acceptable
Training Strategy
Prioritize maintaining or improving performance.
Do not deliberately reduce training intensity simply because calories are lower.
Instead:
• preserve important exercises
• preserve high-quality working sets
• use appropriate RIR
• manage fatigue carefully
• reduce unnecessary volume when recovery deteriorates
Primary indicators:
13. performance
14. waist/body-composition trend
15. recovery
16. bodyweight trend
Do not judge recomposition using bodyweight alone.
--------------------
7. Energy-Limited Training State
Definition
Training performance or recovery is being negatively affected by insufficient energy availability.
Possible signs:
• repeated performance decline
• declining repetitions at similar loads
• reduced training quality
• persistent fatigue
• worsening recovery
• poor sleep
• increased hunger
• reduced motivation
• bodyweight falling faster than intended
Training Strategy
First determine whether the problem is:
• excessive calorie deficit
• insufficient carbohydrate
• excessive training volume
• excessive training frequency
• insufficient recovery
• increased daily activity
Do not immediately add training volume.
Possible intervention order:
17. verify nutrition
18. verify carbohydrate availability
19. verify sleep
20. verify activity
21. reduce unnecessary fatigue
22. adjust training volume if necessary
23. adjust calories when appropriate
When energy availability is limited:
Preserve training quality before increasing training quantity.
--------------------
8. Recovery-Limited Training State
Definition
The athlete has sufficient or apparently sufficient nutrition, but recovery cannot keep up with training stress.
Possible causes:
• excessive weekly volume
• excessive session volume
• high frequency without sufficient recovery
• excessive proximity to failure
• poor exercise selection
• excessive cardio
• insufficient sleep
• psychological stress
• accumulated fatigue
Signs
• repeated performance stagnation
• performance decline
• persistent soreness
• joint irritation
• declining motivation
• poor session quality
• reduced target-muscle connection
• fatigue lasting across sessions
• performance degradation despite adequate nutrition
Intervention
Reduce fatigue before adding stimulus.
Possible adjustments:
• reduce sets
• increase RIR
• reduce failure work
• reduce redundant exercises
• redistribute weekly volume
• increase recovery days
• temporarily reduce frequency
• introduce a deload when appropriate
The system should identify the smallest intervention that restores productive training.
--------------------
9. Plateau / Adaptation-Limited State
Definition
The athlete is not clearly fatigued, but measurable adaptation has slowed or stopped.
Possible pattern:
• performance flat
• body composition flat
• recovery acceptable
• nutrition consistent
• activity consistent
• adherence high
Before changing training, verify:
• actual progression history
• exercise execution
• ROM
• RIR accuracy
• load recording
• sleep
• nutrition
• activity
• bodyweight trend
Intervention Hierarchy
24. verify measurement
25. verify adherence
26. improve exercise execution
27. improve progression strategy
28. modify exercise selection
29. redistribute volume
30. adjust frequency
31. adjust weekly volume
32. reassess nutrition
Avoid changing multiple variables simultaneously.
--------------------
10. Deload / Fatigue-Management State
A deload should not be triggered simply because a fixed number of weeks has passed.
Consider a deload when multiple indicators suggest accumulated fatigue.
Potential indicators:
• repeated performance decline
• persistent soreness
• reduced motivation
• worsening sleep
• joint discomfort
• declining training quality
• unusually high perceived effort
• inability to recover between sessions
Possible deload methods:
Method A — Reduce Volume
Maintain exercises and general intensity while reducing the number of working sets.
Method B — Reduce Proximity to Failure
Keep the normal exercises but increase RIR.
Method C — Reduce Both
Useful when fatigue is substantial.
The appropriate deload depends on the athlete's state.
--------------------
11. Volume Decision Engine
Weekly sets must not be treated as a universal fixed number.
Use the athlete's response to determine whether volume is appropriate.
Evaluate:
• performance trend
• recovery
• target-muscle response
• soreness
• session quality
• current phase
• nutrition
• training age
• exercise selection
If performance is improving
Usually maintain volume.
If performance is improving but target muscle stimulus appears insufficient
Consider a small volume increase.
If performance is stable but recovery is poor
Reduce fatigue before increasing volume.
If performance is declining
Investigate energy and recovery before adding volume.
If performance and recovery are both good but adaptation is stagnant
Consider a controlled training modification.
--------------------
12. Frequency Decision Engine
Training frequency should distribute productive work across the week.
Frequency is not inherently anabolic by itself.
When weekly volume is similar, increasing frequency does not automatically produce greater hypertrophy.
Higher frequency may nevertheless be useful when it:
• improves session quality
• reduces per-session fatigue
• improves technique practice
• distributes high weekly volume
• improves recovery between sessions
Lower frequency may be appropriate when:
• weekly volume is modest
• the athlete responds well to larger sessions
• schedule constraints exist
• recovery is better with fewer sessions
Do not prescribe frequency purely from tradition.
--------------------
13. Intensity and Effort
Most hypertrophy training should be performed sufficiently close to failure to create a strong stimulus while maintaining recoverability.
Use RIR as a practical fatigue-management tool.
General starting framework:
• compounds: commonly around 1–3 RIR
• moderate hypertrophy work: commonly around 1–2 RIR
• isolation work: commonly around 0–2 RIR
These are starting ranges, not universal rules.
Failure can be used strategically, particularly on lower-risk isolation exercises.
Repeated failure across large amounts of training can increase fatigue without necessarily producing proportional additional benefit.
When recovery is poor:
Increase RIR before automatically removing all training.
--------------------
14. Repetition Range
Hypertrophy can be achieved across a relatively broad repetition range when sets are sufficiently challenging and technique is appropriate.
Practical starting ranges:
Heavy compound work
Approximately 5–10 repetitions.
Moderate hypertrophy work
Approximately 8–15 repetitions.
Isolation work
Approximately 10–20 repetitions.
These ranges are not mandatory.
Exercise stability, safety, technique, ROM, fatigue, and individual response may justify different repetition ranges.
--------------------
15. Rest Interval Engine
Rest should be long enough to preserve performance and target-muscle stimulus.
General starting framework:
Compounds
Approximately 2–4+ minutes when needed.
Isolation exercises
Approximately 1–3 minutes depending on exercise and performance.
If performance is falling substantially because rest is too short:
Increase rest before adding volume.
Short rest is not automatically superior for hypertrophy.
--------------------
16. Exercise Selection Engine
Exercise selection should consider:
33. target muscle
34. stable execution
35. ROM
36. resistance profile
37. fatigue cost
38. joint comfort
39. progression potential
40. individual anatomy
41. training skill
42. equipment availability
Do not classify free weights or machines as universally superior.
The preferred exercise is the one that allows the athlete to produce a strong, repeatable stimulus with manageable fatigue and progressive overload.
--------------------
17. Progression Engine
Progression is not limited to increasing weight.
Possible progression variables:
• repetitions
• load
• ROM
• execution quality
• stability
• tempo/control
• RIR at a given load
• total productive work
Double Progression Example
If the target is 8–12 repetitions:
43. begin with a manageable load
44. progress repetitions while maintaining technique
45. once the upper end is reached with appropriate RIR
46. increase load
47. return toward the lower end of the range
48. repeat
The exact progression method may differ by athlete and exercise.
--------------------
18. Training Frequency × Volume Interaction
Do not evaluate frequency independently from volume.
Example:
A high-frequency program may distribute 18 weekly sets over several sessions.
A lower-frequency program may perform the same total work in fewer sessions.
The key questions are:
• Can the athlete recover?
• Does performance remain high?
• Is target-muscle stimulus maintained?
• Is weekly progression occurring?
• Is the schedule sustainable?
Therefore:
Frequency is primarily a distribution variable; weekly productive work and recovery remain central.
--------------------
19. Training × Nutrition Interaction
Training decisions must be interpreted together with nutrition.
Calorie Surplus
Usually greater capacity for:
• training volume
• recovery
• performance progression
But this does not automatically justify more volume.
Maintenance / Recomposition
Prioritize:
• performance retention
• high-quality sets
• recovery management
• controlled fatigue
Calorie Deficit
As the deficit becomes more demanding:
• recovery capacity may decrease
• training volume tolerance may decrease
• performance may become harder to maintain
The first objective becomes:
Preserve muscle and training quality while controlling fatigue.
--------------------
20. Training × Activity Interaction
Daily activity must be considered.
Monitor:
• steps
• cardio
• occupational activity
• spontaneous activity changes
A sudden increase in activity can change energy availability without a deliberate diet change.
Example:
If food intake remains constant but daily steps increase substantially, the athlete may experience:
• slower weight gain
• faster weight loss
• worse recovery
• reduced training performance
Therefore activity changes must be included in the decision engine.
--------------------
21. Muscle-Specific Volume Adjustment
The system should not assume every muscle requires identical training volume.
Evaluate each muscle using:
• performance progression
• target-muscle stimulus
• recovery
• soreness
• visual development
• technique
• exercise selection
• frequency
Possible outcomes:
Under-stimulated
Consider increasing productive work.
Adequately stimulated
Maintain.
Over-fatigued
Reduce or redistribute work.
Technically limited
Improve exercise execution before increasing volume.
--------------------
22. Weak-Point Engine
Weak points should be addressed through targeted training rather than indiscriminate increases in total workload.
Possible interventions:
• increase frequency
• add targeted isolation
• improve exercise selection
• improve ROM
• improve execution
• place priority exercise earlier
• redistribute existing volume
• reduce competing fatigue
The system should prefer redistributing training resources before simply adding large amounts of volume.
--------------------
23. High-Frequency Training Framework
High frequency can work well when each session is controlled.
Requirements:
• manageable per-session volume
• controlled proximity to failure
• stable exercise execution
• sufficient recovery
• clear progression
• fatigue monitoring
High frequency does NOT mean:
train every muscle hard every day.
The key variable is the total recoverable training stimulus.
--------------------
24. State Transition Rules
Training state should be reassessed continuously.
Productive → Recovery-Limited
Possible transition:
• performance decline
• fatigue accumulation
• recovery deterioration
Productive → Energy-Limited
Possible transition:
• calorie deficit increases
• bodyweight falls too quickly
• performance declines
Productive → Plateau
Possible transition:
• performance stops progressing
• recovery remains adequate
• adherence remains high
Recomposition → Productive Gaining
Possible transition:
• calorie intake increases
• bodyweight begins rising gradually
• performance improves
Recomposition → Energy-Limited
Possible transition:
• deficit becomes excessive
• performance and recovery decline
Recovery-Limited → Productive
Possible transition:
• fatigue reduced
• recovery restored
• performance stabilizes
--------------------
25. Minimum-Change Principle
When the athlete is progressing:
Change as little as necessary.
When the athlete is not progressing:
Change the smallest number of variables necessary to identify the limiting factor.
Avoid changing simultaneously:
• calories
• carbohydrates
• training volume
• frequency
• exercise selection
• cardio
• steps
unless there is a clear reason.
Otherwise the system cannot identify which intervention caused the result.
--------------------

27. Evidence Integration
Training decisions should combine three evidence sources:
Scientific Evidence
Used to establish general principles.
Examples:
• resistance-training volume
• frequency
• proximity to failure
• repetition range
• rest intervals
• hypertrophy mechanisms
Natural Athlete Case Evidence
Used to provide real-world examples of how successful natural trainees organize training.
Use:
• documented training
• documented preparation
• documented nutrition
• longitudinal bodyweight
• documented competition history
Do not treat athlete practice as proof that the practice caused the result.
Individual Response
The athlete's own longitudinal data has priority when making individualized adjustments.
Therefore:
Scientific evidence establishes the framework; natural athlete cases provide practical models; individual response determines personalization.

Every athlete-specific claim should be classified when possible.
DOCUMENTED
Directly supported by a reliable public source.
ATHLETE-REPORTED

31. Training State Decision Tree
Use the following sequence:
DATA↓BODYWEIGHT TREND↓NUTRITION / ENERGY AVAILABILITY↓PERFORMANCE TREND↓RECOVERY↓TRAINING LOAD↓TRAINING STATE↓LIMITING FACTOR↓MINIMUM EFFECTIVE CHANGE↓MONITOR↓REASSESS
Never skip directly from:
"muscle growth is slow"
to:
"add more sets."
First identify the limiting factor.
--------------------
32. Core Rule
The training system should continuously answer:
Is the athlete receiving enough stimulus to progress, and can the athlete recover from that stimulus?
If stimulus is insufficient:
increase or redistribute productive training.
If stimulus is sufficient and progress is occurring:
maintain.
If stimulus is excessive:
reduce or redistribute fatigue.
If nutrition is limiting:
address energy availability.
If recovery is limiting:
reduce fatigue and improve recovery.
If adaptation is limited:
modify the training stimulus systematically.
The final objective is:
Maximum sustainable hypertrophy stimulus with minimum unnecessary fatigue.
