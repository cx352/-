
Cutting Engine
1. Purpose
This module manages fat-loss phases while prioritizing:
• muscle retention
• training performance
• recovery
• sustainable rate of weight loss
• adherence
• long-term body composition
The system must not treat faster weight loss as automatically better.
The objective is:
Reduce body fat while preserving as much lean mass, training performance, and recovery capacity as reasonably possible.
--------------------

• psychological and physical tolerance

4. Rate of Weight Loss
Rate of weight loss should be individualized.
A useful starting framework for resistance-trained individuals is approximately:
0.25–0.75% of bodyweight per week.
This is a starting range, not a mandatory target.
The appropriate rate depends on:
• starting body-fat level
• leanness
• training age
• muscle mass
• competition timeline
• recovery
• previous dieting history
As the athlete becomes leaner, slower weight loss is generally more appropriate.
--------------------
5. Mild Fat Loss
Definition
A relatively small calorie deficit intended to reduce body fat while maintaining high training quality.
Typical characteristics:
• small calorie deficit
• slow weight loss
• stable or improving performance
• manageable hunger
• good recovery
This approach is particularly useful when:
• body-fat level is moderate
• there is no urgent competition deadline
• muscle retention is a high priority
• the athlete wants gradual recomposition
--------------------
6. Moderate Fat Loss
Use when:
• body-fat reduction is a clear priority
• mild dieting has become too slow
• adherence is good
• recovery remains manageable
Possible outcomes:
• faster weight loss
• greater hunger
• greater recovery demands
Training should be monitored carefully.
--------------------
7. Aggressive Fat Loss
Aggressive dieting should be reserved for situations where the faster rate is justified.
Possible situations:
• short competition timeline
• medically supervised weight-loss context
• temporary short-term requirement
Potential disadvantages:
• greater fatigue
• reduced training performance
• greater hunger
• reduced recovery
• increased risk of lean-mass loss
The system should not default to aggressive dieting.
--------------------
8. Recomposition Cutting Strategy
When the athlete wants simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain:
Primary indicators become:
6. training performance
7. waist measurement
8. bodyweight trend
9. visual changes
10. recovery
A stable bodyweight with:
• decreasing waist
• improving performance
• improving physique
may indicate successful recomposition.
Do not automatically reduce calories further just because scale weight is not falling.
--------------------
9. Protein
Protein intake should generally remain high during fat-loss phases.
A practical evidence-based framework:
Approximately 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day for most resistance-trained individuals.
Higher protein intake may be useful during aggressive dieting or when the athlete is already lean, but individual context matters.
Protein should be distributed across the day rather than concentrated into a single meal.
--------------------
10. Carbohydrate
Carbohydrates should be adjusted according to:
• training performance
• calorie target
• training frequency
• activity
• preference
• recovery
Do not remove carbohydrates automatically.
When training performance is falling during a deficit, investigate:
• total calories
• carbohydrate availability
• sleep
• recovery
• excessive volume
• excessive cardio
before making a large training change.
--------------------
11. Dietary Fat
Dietary fat should remain sufficient for:
• dietary adherence
• food enjoyment
• normal physiological function
• nutrient absorption
Avoid unnecessarily reducing fat to extremely low levels.
The remaining calories can be distributed between carbohydrate and fat according to:
• performance
• preference
• digestion
• adherence
--------------------
12. Calorie Adjustment Engine
Do not adjust calories based on one weigh-in.
Use:
• 7-day average
• 14-day trend
• 28-day trend
If weight is falling too slowly
First verify:
• actual food intake
• adherence
• step count
• cardio
• water fluctuations
Then consider a small calorie reduction or activity increase.
If weight is falling at the intended rate
Maintain.
If weight is falling too quickly
Consider:
• increasing calories
• reducing cardio
• reducing unnecessary activity
• reducing the size of the deficit
especially if performance and recovery are deteriorating.
--------------------
13. Calorie Adjustment Size
Prefer small adjustments.
A practical starting adjustment is approximately:
100–250 kcal/day.
The exact adjustment depends on:
• body size
• current deficit
• rate of weight change
• activity
• recovery
• training performance
Avoid repeated large calorie changes.
After an adjustment:
monitor for approximately 7–14 days before making another major change, unless there is a clear reason to act sooner.
--------------------
14. Training During a Cut
The primary goal is to preserve muscle and training quality.
Important principles:
• maintain meaningful resistance-training intensity
• preserve key exercises where possible
• maintain high-quality working sets
• avoid unnecessary volume increases
• manage proximity to failure
• reduce fatigue when recovery deteriorates
A calorie deficit does not automatically mean that training intensity must become very light.
--------------------
15. Training Volume During a Cut
Do not automatically cut training volume when calories decrease.
If:
• performance is stable
• recovery is good
• sleep is good
• motivation is good
then existing volume may be maintained.
If recovery deteriorates:
11. remove redundant sets
12. reduce failure work
13. reduce unnecessary isolation volume
14. redistribute weekly volume
15. reduce frequency if necessary
Preserve the highest-quality training stimulus first.
--------------------
16. Cardio Engine
Cardio is a tool, not a requirement.
Possible benefits:
• increases energy expenditure
• improves cardiovascular fitness
• provides additional activity
But excessive cardio can interfere with:
• recovery
• leg training
• appetite control
• adherence
Use the minimum amount required to achieve the desired rate of progress.
--------------------
17. Step Count
Daily steps should be monitored because they influence energy expenditure.
If the athlete normally averages:
7,000–8,000 steps/day
and suddenly increases to:
12,000–15,000 steps/day
the calorie deficit may become substantially larger without any diet change.
Therefore:
Activity consistency is important when interpreting weight trends.
--------------------
18. Weight-Fluctuation Engine
Short-term bodyweight changes can result from:
• glycogen
• water
• sodium
• food volume
• bowel contents
• inflammation
• training stress
Therefore:
Do not interpret a single day's weight as a change in body fat.
Use moving averages and multi-day trends.
--------------------
19. Waist Measurement
Waist measurement is an important secondary indicator.
Possible pattern:
Weight down + waist down
Likely successful fat loss.
Weight stable + waist down
Possible recomposition or water/glycogen changes.
Weight down + waist stable
Investigate measurement noise, water changes, and lean-mass changes.
Weight stable + waist increasing
Investigate calorie intake, activity, and adherence.
Never diagnose body-composition changes from waist alone.
--------------------
20. Performance Protection
Training performance is a major muscle-retention signal.
Monitor:
• load
• repetitions
• RIR
• technique
• ROM
• exercise quality
Small fluctuations are normal.
Persistent deterioration across multiple sessions is more meaningful.
If performance repeatedly declines:
16. verify sleep
17. verify calories
18. verify carbohydrate
19. verify hydration
20. verify training fatigue
21. verify cardio/activity
22. reduce training fatigue if necessary
--------------------
21. Recovery Protection
During fat loss, monitor:
• sleep
• fatigue
• soreness
• motivation
• hunger
• stress
• joint condition
If recovery deteriorates while weight loss is rapid:
The deficit may be too aggressive.
The system should consider slowing the rate of weight loss before increasing training stress.
--------------------
22. Plateau Engine
A plateau should not be diagnosed from several days of stable weight.
Before changing calories, check:
• 7-day average
• 14-day trend
• 28-day trend
• food tracking accuracy
• step count
• cardio
• sodium
• carbohydrate intake
• menstrual cycle when relevant
• bowel movements
• training inflammation
For male athletes, also consider:
• glycogen fluctuations
• high-sodium meals
• unusually high carbohydrate intake
• increased training volume
If the actual fat-loss trend has stalled for long enough and adherence is high:
make one controlled adjustment.
--------------------
23. Diet Break
A diet break may be considered when:
• dieting duration is long
• fatigue is accumulating
• hunger is high
• training performance is deteriorating
• adherence is becoming difficult
A diet break generally means returning toward estimated maintenance calories rather than deliberately overeating.
Its main purposes are:
• improve adherence
• reduce diet fatigue
• support training
• improve psychological sustainability
A diet break should not be presented as a guaranteed metabolic reset.
--------------------
24. Refeed
A refeed can be used as a short-term dietary strategy, particularly when:
• training performance is temporarily poor
• carbohydrate intake is very low
• the athlete finds higher-carbohydrate days useful for adherence
A refeed is not inherently required for fat loss.
Its main practical function is often:
• carbohydrate availability
• training support
• diet adherence
--------------------
25. Post-Contest Cutting State
A recently finished bodybuilding competition requires a different framework.
Do not immediately continue aggressive dieting simply because bodyweight increases after the show.
Early post-contest weight gain can reflect:
• glycogen restoration
• increased food volume
• increased intestinal contents
• sodium changes
• water restoration
Therefore:
Post-contest bodyweight increase is not automatically equivalent to fat regain.
The system must distinguish:
• physiological recovery
• normal restoration
• excessive fat gain
--------------------
26. Very Lean Athlete Rule
As body fat becomes very low:
• weight-loss rate should generally become slower
• recovery becomes increasingly important
• training fatigue becomes more consequential
• muscle retention becomes harder
Therefore the system should become increasingly conservative with:
• calorie deficits
• cardio
• training volume
as the athlete approaches very lean condition.
--------------------

31. Core Rules
23. Never judge fat loss from one weigh-in.
24. Never make a large calorie change without sufficient trend data.
25. Never sacrifice resistance-training quality unnecessarily.
26. Do not assume faster weight loss means better results.
27. Protect muscle and performance.
28. Keep protein sufficiently high.
29. Maintain reasonable activity consistency.
30. Use cardio strategically.
31. Adjust one major variable at a time whenever possible.
32. Use the athlete's own response to personalize the plan.
33. Distinguish documented evidence from assumptions.
34. Natural athlete case studies are practical references, not causal proof.
35. The leaner the athlete becomes, the more conservative the system should become.
36. Recomposition should be judged using multiple indicators rather than bodyweight alone.
Final principle:
The best cutting strategy is the smallest sustainable deficit that produces the desired body-composition change while preserving training performance, muscle mass, recovery, and adherence.
