
Recovery Engine
1. Purpose
This module evaluates whether the athlete can recover from the current combination of:
• resistance training
• nutrition
• daily activity
• cardio
• psychological stress
• sleep
• life demands
The objective is not to maximize recovery metrics.
The objective is:
Maintain sufficient recovery capacity to support productive training and long-term adaptation.
Recovery is not an isolated variable.
It is the result of the interaction between:
TRAINING LOAD+ENERGY AVAILABILITY+SLEEP+ACTIVITY+LIFE STRESS+INDIVIDUAL RECOVERY CAPACITY

3. Recovery State Classification
Classify the athlete into:
1. Well Recovered
2. Adequately Recovered
3. Mildly Recovery-Limited
4. Moderately Recovery-Limited
5. Severely Recovery-Limited
6. Acute Illness / Injury State
The classification should be based on multiple indicators.
Do not diagnose recovery from a single symptom.
--------------------
4. Well Recovered
Typical signs:
• performance progressing
• sleep adequate
• soreness manageable
• motivation normal
• appetite appropriate
• no persistent joint irritation
• sessions feel productive
• recovery between sessions adequate
Decision
Usually maintain the current training and nutrition strategy.
Do not add training simply because recovery appears good.
--------------------
5. Adequately Recovered
The athlete is recovering sufficiently for continued progress.
Possible pattern:
• minor soreness
• occasional fatigue
• normal performance fluctuations
• acceptable sleep
• stable motivation
Decision
Maintain.
Monitor for changes.
No intervention is required unless the trend deteriorates.
--------------------
6. Mildly Recovery-Limited
Possible signs:
• repeated mild fatigue
• reduced motivation
• slightly worse sleep
• soreness lasting longer
• slower performance progression
• increased perceived effort
Possible causes:
• slightly excessive volume
• insufficient carbohydrate
• mild calorie deficit
• increased steps
• increased cardio
• life stress
• reduced sleep
Intervention
Identify the most likely limiting factor.
Possible changes:
• improve sleep
• reduce redundant sets
• increase RIR slightly
• reduce cardio
• restore calories if appropriate
• reduce activity if unusually high
Prefer the smallest intervention.
--------------------
7. Moderately Recovery-Limited
Possible signs:
• performance declining across several sessions
• persistent fatigue
• poor sleep
• high soreness
• declining motivation
• training quality worsening
• recovery not completed before the next session
Intervention Priority
7. Check sleep
8. Check calorie intake
9. Check carbohydrate
10. Check activity
11. Check cardio
12. Check training volume
13. Check proximity to failure
14. Reduce unnecessary fatigue
Potential intervention:
• reduce training volume
• increase RIR
• reduce failure work
• reduce cardio
• increase calories if the deficit is excessive
• temporarily increase recovery days
--------------------
8. Severely Recovery-Limited
Possible signs:
• repeated substantial performance decline
• persistent exhaustion
• severe sleep deterioration
• inability to complete normal training
• significant joint pain
• illness symptoms
• inability to recover between sessions
Decision
The priority shifts from progression to recovery.
Potential actions:
• substantially reduce training stress
• take additional rest
• reduce cardio
• restore energy availability
• prioritize sleep
• seek appropriate professional medical evaluation when symptoms warrant it
The system must not encourage the athlete to "push through" serious symptoms.
--------------------
9. Sleep Engine
Sleep is one of the highest-priority recovery variables.
Monitor:
• duration
• consistency
• quality
• timing
• interruptions
A temporary poor night does not automatically require a program change.
Repeated poor sleep should influence training decisions.
Persistent Sleep Reduction
If sleep remains poor for multiple days:
consider:
• reducing training volume
• increasing RIR
• reducing cardio
• improving schedule
• reducing unnecessary late-day stimulation
• evaluating overall stress
Do not compensate for chronic sleep loss by simply adding stimulants.
--------------------
10. Training Fatigue Engine
Training fatigue can accumulate through:
• high weekly volume
• high session volume
• high frequency
• frequent failure training
• excessive eccentric stress
• excessive cardio
• poor exercise selection
• insufficient recovery
Monitor:
• performance
• RIR
• soreness
• motivation
• session quality
If fatigue rises while performance falls:
reduce fatigue before increasing stimulus.
--------------------
11. Volume × Recovery
More volume is not automatically better.
Evaluate whether additional sets produce:
• additional performance
• better target-muscle stimulus
• measurable adaptation
without causing disproportionate fatigue.
Productive Volume
Signs:
• performance stable/improving
• recovery acceptable
• target muscle responding
Excessive Volume
Possible signs:
• performance stagnation
• persistent soreness
• poor recovery
• declining motivation
• reduced session quality
When excessive volume is suspected:
remove low-value work before removing the highest-value exercises.
--------------------
12. Frequency × Recovery
Higher frequency can be useful when it distributes training stress.
However, frequency can become problematic when:
• sessions overlap heavily
• the same muscle is repeatedly fatigued
• recovery time becomes insufficient
• training quality decreases
Do not change frequency simply because a certain number of sessions is considered optimal.
Evaluate the athlete's actual response.
--------------------
13. Failure Training × Recovery
Training to failure can increase fatigue.
Use failure selectively.
Higher failure exposure may be appropriate for:
• stable isolation exercises
• low-risk movements
• final sets
Use more caution with:
• heavy compounds
• technically demanding exercises
• high-volume programs
If recovery deteriorates:
reduce failure exposure before removing all resistance training.
--------------------
14. Exercise Selection × Recovery
Two exercises targeting the same muscle can create very different fatigue costs.
Consider:
• systemic fatigue
• local fatigue
• joint stress
• stability requirements
• technical complexity
• ROM
• resistance profile
The best exercise is not necessarily the exercise that produces the highest absolute load.
Prefer exercises that provide:
high-quality target-muscle stimulus with manageable fatigue.
--------------------
15. Nutrition × Recovery
Recovery capacity depends partly on energy availability.
Energy Surplus
Usually provides greater capacity for:
• recovery
• performance
• training volume
Maintenance / Recomposition
Recovery may remain good but has less energy margin.
Monitor fatigue carefully.
Energy Deficit
Recovery capacity may decrease as the deficit becomes larger.
Potential signs:
• declining performance
• increased hunger
• poor sleep
• persistent fatigue
• reduced motivation
If these occur:
investigate whether the deficit is too aggressive.
--------------------
16. Carbohydrate × Recovery
Carbohydrate can support:
• training performance
• glycogen restoration
• high-volume training
• perceived energy
If training quality deteriorates during a deficit:
check carbohydrate availability before assuming that more training is required.
A small carbohydrate increase may sometimes be preferable to a large reduction in training.
--------------------
17. Protein × Recovery
Protein supports:
• muscle retention
• muscle remodeling
• satiety
A practical general range:
Approximately 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day.
Protein should be evaluated together with total calorie intake and training demand.
--------------------
18. Activity × Recovery
Daily activity is part of total workload.
Monitor:
• steps
• cardio
• occupational activity
Example:
If training stays unchanged but steps increase substantially, total fatigue and energy expenditure may increase.
Therefore:
Activity changes must be considered before changing the resistance-training program.
--------------------
19. Psychological Stress
Psychological stress can affect:
• sleep
• motivation
• perceived fatigue
• training quality
• adherence
Do not assume that all fatigue originates from training.
If life stress increases substantially:
the appropriate response may be to temporarily reduce training stress rather than increase discipline.
--------------------
20. Recovery and Training Performance
Performance is one of the most useful practical recovery indicators.
Monitor:
• load
• repetitions
• RIR
• technique
• ROM
• session quality
Single-session performance changes are not sufficient.
Look for trends across multiple sessions.
--------------------
21. Morning Readiness
Subjective morning readiness can be tracked using:
• energy
• motivation
• soreness
• sleep quality
• perceived fatigue
A simple 1–5 scale may be used.
Example:
1 = extremely poor2 = poor3 = normal4 = good5 = excellent
This is a monitoring tool, not a medical diagnostic system.
--------------------
22. Recovery Score
A simple internal monitoring score may combine:
• sleep
• fatigue
• soreness
• motivation
• performance
However:
The score should not override objective training and body-composition trends.
Example:
A low subjective score with normal performance may not require a major intervention.
A low score combined with declining performance for several sessions is more meaningful.
--------------------
23. Acute vs Chronic Fatigue
Distinguish between:
Acute Fatigue
Temporary fatigue after a hard session.
Usually resolves within normal recovery time.
Accumulated Fatigue
Fatigue persists across multiple sessions.
Potential signs:
• repeated performance decline
• persistent soreness
• poor sleep
• reduced motivation
Chronic Recovery Problem
Long-lasting deterioration that does not resolve with normal recovery.
This may require broader investigation.
Do not automatically label prolonged fatigue as "overtraining."
--------------------
24. Deload Decision
A deload should be considered when multiple fatigue indicators accumulate.
Possible triggers:
• persistent performance decline
• high soreness
• poor sleep
• low motivation
• joint irritation
• unusually high RPE
• inability to recover between sessions
Do not schedule deloads purely according to a fixed calendar.
--------------------
25. Deload Methods
Volume Reduction
Reduce working sets while maintaining normal movement patterns.
RIR Increase
Perform the same exercises with more repetitions in reserve.
Combined Deload
Reduce both volume and proximity to failure.
Frequency Reduction
Temporarily reduce sessions when recovery is significantly impaired.
Select the smallest intervention that restores recovery.
--------------------
26. Return From Deload
Do not immediately return to maximum training stress.
Resume gradually.
Monitor:
• performance
• soreness
• sleep
• motivation
• recovery
If performance rebounds and recovery improves:
progressively return to normal training.
--------------------
27. Recovery in a Calorie Deficit
As the deficit becomes larger:
• fatigue may rise
• training volume tolerance may fall
• sleep may worsen
• performance may decline
Therefore:
The training system should become more conservative as the energy deficit becomes more demanding.
Muscle retention takes priority over maximizing training volume.
--------------------
28. Recovery During a Bulk
A calorie surplus can increase recovery capacity.
However:
More food does not make unlimited training stress recoverable.
Monitor:
• performance
• soreness
• joint health
• motivation
• sleep
If recovery is poor during a bulk:
investigate training load before simply adding calories.
--------------------
29. Recovery During Recomposition
Recomposition creates a narrower energy margin than a clear calorie surplus.
Priorities:
• maintain high-quality resistance training
• avoid unnecessary cardio
• maintain sufficient protein
• manage fatigue
• maintain adequate sleep
If recovery deteriorates:
consider reducing training stress before increasing the deficit.
--------------------
30. Recovery During Contest Prep
As the athlete becomes leaner:
recovery becomes increasingly important.
Monitor:
• sleep
• fatigue
• training performance
• soreness
• hunger
• motivation
• activity
Potential intervention:
• reduce unnecessary volume
• increase RIR
• reduce cardio
• adjust calories
• increase recovery days
The objective is muscle retention and stage readiness.
--------------------
31. Recovery During Post-Contest Phase
Post-contest athletes may experience:
• high hunger
• low energy availability
• accumulated fatigue
• reduced training performance
• psychological diet fatigue
Priorities:
15. restore energy availability
16. restore training performance
17. improve sleep
18. normalize activity
19. manage bodyweight restoration
20. transition to sustainable nutrition
Do not interpret all post-contest fatigue as a training-program problem.
--------------------
32. Recovery Intervention Hierarchy
When recovery deteriorates, investigate in this order:
21. sleep
22. calorie availability
23. carbohydrate availability
24. activity
25. cardio
26. training volume
27. failure exposure
28. training frequency
29. exercise selection
30. life stress
This is not a rigid sequence.
If one factor is clearly responsible, address it directly.
--------------------
33. Minimum-Change Principle
If recovery is acceptable:
Do not make unnecessary changes.
If recovery deteriorates:
Identify the most likely limiting factor and make the smallest effective intervention.
Avoid simultaneously changing:
• calories
• carbs
• training volume
• frequency
• cardio
• steps
• exercise selection
unless the situation clearly requires multiple interventions.
--------------------

37. Data Classification
Recovery-related athlete information must be classified as:
DOCUMENTED
Directly supported by reliable public evidence.
ATHLETE-REPORTED
Directly stated by the athlete.
OFFICIAL
Supported by an official organization or competition record.
SECONDARY
Reported by another source.
DERIVED
Calculated or inferred.
UNKNOWN
Insufficient evidence.
Do not convert UNKNOWN into fact.

39. Safety Boundary
This system is a fitness decision-support framework.
It must not diagnose:
• medical conditions
• endocrine disorders
• eating disorders
• overtraining syndrome
• injuries
If an athlete has:
• persistent unexplained fatigue
• chest pain
• fainting
• severe shortness of breath
• significant injury
• persistent illness symptoms
• major unexplained performance decline
appropriate medical evaluation should be recommended.
--------------------
40. Core Rules
31. Recovery is the interaction between training, nutrition, sleep, activity, and stress.
32. Do not diagnose recovery from one bad workout.
33. Do not add training simply because recovery appears good.
34. When recovery deteriorates, identify the limiting factor before changing the entire program.
35. Reduce unnecessary fatigue before removing high-value training.
36. Use RIR as a fatigue-management tool.
37. Failure should be used strategically.
38. Activity is part of total workload.
39. A calorie deficit can reduce recovery capacity.
40. More food does not make unlimited training recoverable.
41. Sleep is a major recovery variable.
42. Psychological stress can influence training recovery.
43. Deloads should respond to accumulated fatigue rather than an arbitrary calendar.
44. Use the smallest effective intervention.
45. Monitor trends rather than isolated measurements.
46. Individual response takes priority when personal longitudinal data are available.
47. Natural athlete cases provide practical examples, not universal prescriptions.
48. Medical symptoms should not be handled as ordinary training fatigue.
Final principle:
The best recovery strategy is not maximum rest. It is the minimum recovery support required to sustain high-quality, progressive, and recoverable training.
